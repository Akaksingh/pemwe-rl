\begin{abstract}
% Write LAST, after Results. One sentence each: problem, gap, method, result, caveat.
Hydrogen production from proton exchange membrane (PEM) water electrolysis coupled
directly to variable renewable generation is limited less by conversion efficiency than by
stack degradation: the on/off cycling and fast ramping that follow the resource are
precisely the operating patterns that shorten stack life. We formulate real-time
electrolyzer setpoint control as a Markov decision process at a one-minute control
interval and train a reinforcement learning agent to trade hydrogen yield against
cumulative degradation, against a load-following rule-based controller drawn from the
literature. Sweeping the degradation weight traces a frontier spanning \SI{22.3}{\percent}
in cumulative degradation against \SI{2.1}{\percent} in yield, reaching a projected
stack life of \SI{7.39}{\year} against \SI{6.20}{\year} for the best rule-based
controller, for \SI{3.9}{\percent} less hydrogen. The learned policy does not dominate
a well-tuned slew limit at the operating point they share; its contribution is to reach
degradation rates no rule-based controller attains, which it does by reducing ON/OFF
cycling from \num{22.9} to \num{2.0} events per day and paying for that with deliberate
curtailment. Obtaining this required an off-policy, entropy-regularised algorithm: PPO
on the identical environment and budget did not respond to the degradation weight at all.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
PEM water electrolysis, reinforcement learning, degradation, green hydrogen,
real-time control, variable renewable energy

\section{Introduction}
Electrolytic hydrogen produced directly from variable renewable generation is central to
current decarbonisation policy. India's National Green Hydrogen Mission targets
five million tonnes of annual production by 2030, and the hybrid solar--wind resource of
Kutch, Gujarat --- the site studied here --- is among the regions being developed for it.
Coupling an electrolyzer directly to such a resource, without grid firming or large
intermediate storage, is attractive economically but places the stack under an operating
regime it was not designed for.

The binding constraint in that regime is not conversion efficiency but stack lifetime.
Degradation in proton exchange membrane (PEM) water electrolysis is well
characterised~\cite{feng2017pemwe,bernhardt2026degradation}, and a distinct body of
evidence now separates \emph{intermittency-driven} degradation from steady-state
ageing~\cite{intermittency2025}: on/off cycling, ohmic resistance rise, and thermal fatigue
driven by fast ramping. These mechanisms are consequences of \emph{how} the stack is
operated, not merely how long, which is precisely what makes them a control problem.

This reframes the control objective. The conventional strategy --- track the available
resource up to the rated limit and idle below a threshold~\cite{pvt_control_2026} ---
maximises instantaneous hydrogen output and, by construction, also maximises exposure to
every mechanism above. Two properties of the plant make the resulting trade-off non-trivial
rather than merely conservative. First, efficiency is non-monotonic in power: hydrogen
crossover dominates at low current density and voltage losses at high current density, so
the setpoint maximising hydrogen \emph{rate} is not the one maximising hydrogen \emph{per
unit energy}. Second, idling is not free, because the anode sits at open-circuit potential
where iridium dissolution proceeds~\cite{yu2020iridium}. A controller therefore cannot
minimise degradation simply by running less.

Degradation-aware operation has been addressed with predictive
control~\cite{mpc_refuel_2026}, and reinforcement learning has been applied both to
alkaline electrolysers under renewable input~\cite{awe_rl_2025} and to PEM scheduling under
constraints~\cite{drl_scheduling_2026}. Each of these satisfies two of the three properties
this problem requires --- real-time setpoint control, a PEM device, and an explicit
degradation cost in the objective --- but none satisfies all three
(Section~\ref{sec:related}).

We make the following contributions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item We formulate PEM electrolyzer setpoint control as a Markov decision process at a
        \textbf{one-minute control interval}, resolving the cycling and ramping mechanisms
        that hourly scheduling formulations cannot represent, and we train SAC and PPO
        agents against it on measured resource data.
  \item We couple a five-term degradation model to the polarization curve so that
        accumulated degradation reduces future yield, and we \textbf{calibrate it against a
        published stack lifetime} rather than choosing its coefficients by hand: the
        literature rule-based controller, driven by the measured resource, is required to
        reproduce the reported service life of a real PEM stack.
  \item We characterise the learned controller as a \textbf{yield--degradation frontier}
        traced by the degradation weight, rather than as a single operating point. This
        reports the trade-off the controller can actually make, and it does not depend on
        the learned policy winning on any one axis.
  \item We show that the published load-following rule is \textbf{Pareto-dominated by
        removing its shutdown threshold alone} --- \SI{1.6}{\percent} more hydrogen and
        \SI{14.7}{\percent} less degradation at once --- which quantifies how much of the
        available improvement requires no learning, and sets a substantially stronger
        baseline for the comparison that follows.
\end{itemize}

The study is in simulation; Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states the consequences of that
plainly.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Degradation in PEM water electrolysis}
Degradation mechanisms in PEM water electrolysis are well catalogued. Feng
\emph{et al.}~\cite{feng2017pemwe} review membrane thinning, catalyst dissolution and
transport-layer passivation, and a recent survey~\cite{bernhardt2026degradation} extends
this across the cell stack together with the diagnostics used to separate the mechanisms.
Microscopic studies of iridium dissolution and redeposition~\cite{yu2020iridium} establish
that the anode is not benign at open circuit, which is why an idle state cannot be treated
as free in any control formulation. Quantitatively, a review of degradation models for
power allocation~\cite{degmodels2025} reports membrane thinning of order
\SI{0.1}{\micro\meter\per\kilo\hour} and catalyst active-area losses approaching
\SI{20}{\percent} over \SI{500}{\hour} under stress conditions.

Most relevant here, a survey of dynamic degradation under renewable
intermittency~\cite{intermittency2025} isolates the mechanisms specific to variable
operation --- on/off cycling, ohmic resistance rise bounded by roughly
\SI{50}{\micro\volt\per\hour}, and thermal fatigue driven by ramping --- and it is these,
rather than steady-state ageing, that a setpoint controller can actually influence.

\subsection{Control of electrolyzers under variable supply}
The simplest and most widely deployed strategy is load following: track the available
resource up to the rated limit and idle below a threshold. We adopt the formulation of
Ref.~\cite{pvt_control_2026} verbatim as our baseline, so that the comparison is against a
published control law rather than one constructed for the purpose.

Degradation-aware control has been addressed with predictive methods.
Ref.~\cite{mpc_refuel_2026} reports that power smoothing under a predictive controller
extends PEM stack life from approximately five to seven and a half years. That work is the
closest non-learning counterpart to ours: it shares the objective but not the method, and
it supplies the lifetime figure against which we calibrate our degradation model
(Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).

\subsection{Reinforcement learning for electrolysis}
Reinforcement learning is now established across power and energy
systems~\cite{cao2020rlreview}. Two studies sit closest to this work, and each differs on
exactly one axis.

Ref.~\cite{awe_rl_2025} trains an RL agent against a physics-informed surrogate to
optimise temperature, flow and current density under renewable input. The method is close
to ours, but the device is a zero-gap \emph{alkaline} electrolyser, whose degradation
physics and dynamic response differ from PEM, and the reward targets efficiency without an
explicit degradation term.

Ref.~\cite{drl_scheduling_2026} applies deep RL to a PEM electrolyzer in an
electric--hydrogen station under a constrained MDP. The device is correct and the method is
RL, but the granularity is \emph{scheduling} --- hour-ahead commitment with discrete
actions --- rather than real-time setpoint control. This distinction is the reason our
control interval is one minute rather than one hour: at hourly resolution the cycling and
ramping mechanisms of Ref.~\cite{intermittency2025} are not resolved at all, and the
control problem reduces to a scheduling problem.

\subsection{Position of this work}
The contribution is the intersection of three properties that prior work satisfies only
two at a time: real-time setpoint control at a sub-hourly interval, a PEM rather than
alkaline device, and an explicit degradation cost inside the reward.
Table~\ref{tab:position} summarises.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Plant model}
We model a \SI{1}{\mega\watt}-class PEM electrolyzer of 280 cells, each
\SI{1000}{\centi\meter\squared}, rated at
$j_\mathrm{r}=\SI{2.0}{\ampere\per\centi\meter\squared}$ and \SI{60}{\celsius}, operated in
differential-pressure mode with the cathode at \SI{30}{\bar} and the anode near ambient.
Cell voltage follows the Amphlett form
\begin{equation}
V_\mathrm{cell}(j) = E_\mathrm{rev}(T,p) + V_\mathrm{act}(j) + V_\mathrm{ohm}(j)
                     + V_\mathrm{conc}(j) + \dVdeg ,
\label{eq:vcell}
\end{equation}
where $\dVdeg$ is the lumped degradation state of Section~\ref{sec:degmodel}. The
reversible voltage carries the usual linear temperature correction plus a Nernst pressure
term, giving $E_\mathrm{rev}=\SI{1.246}{\volt}$ at the operating point, of which
\SI{48.8}{\milli\volt} is the thermodynamic cost of evolving hydrogen against the
pressurised cathode.

Activation losses use the symmetric Butler--Volmer relation inverted as
$\eta=(RT/\alpha F)\,\mathrm{asinh}(j/2j_0)$ at both electrodes. Rather than adopt transfer
coefficients directly, we fix them through the Tafel slope $b=2.303\,RT/\alpha F$ they imply
at \SI{60}{\celsius}, since that is the quantity experimental work reports:
$\alpha_\mathrm{a}=1.5$ gives \SI{44}{\milli\volt\per\deci\bel} per decade, inside the
40--60\,mV/dec reported for oxygen evolution on
IrO\textsubscript{2}~\cite{feng2017pemwe,yu2020iridium}, and $\alpha_\mathrm{c}=2.0$ gives
33\,mV/dec, consistent with the $\approx$30\,mV/dec of hydrogen evolution on platinum.

Ohmic loss and hydrogen crossover are deliberately derived from a \emph{single} membrane
description. For a \SI{70}{\micro\meter} reinforced perfluorosulfonic-acid membrane at
\SI{0.10}{\siemens\per\centi\meter} with \SI{0.030}{\ohm\centi\meter\squared} of contact,
porous-transport-layer and bipolar-plate resistance, the area-specific resistance is
\SI{0.100}{\ohm\centi\meter\squared}. The same thickness $\delta$ sets the hydrogen
permeation flux, expressed as an equivalent crossover current density by Fick's law,
\begin{equation}
j_\mathrm{cross} = 2F\,P_{\mathrm{H_2}}\,p_\mathrm{cat} / \delta
                 = \SI{0.0165}{\ampere\per\centi\meter\squared},
\label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
so that the Faradaic efficiency is $\eta_F = 1 - j_\mathrm{cross}/j$. Because crossover is
a current-independent leak, it consumes a larger \emph{fraction} of the product the lower
the current density: a thinner membrane buys efficiency at high current and loses it at low
current. Tying both terms to one thickness means the two ends of the efficiency curve
cannot be tuned independently, so the trade-off the controller faces is a property of the
membrane rather than of our parameter choices.

Hydrogen production follows Faraday's law derated by $\eta_F$. A constant balance-of-plant
parasitic of \SI{1.5}{\percent} of rated (circulation pumps, chiller, controls, gas drying)
is drawn whenever the stack is on, so reported efficiencies are \emph{system} rather than
stack figures. Rated plant power is therefore \SI{1049}{\kilo\watt}, and the plant cannot
run below \SI{36.8}{\kilo\watt} (\SI{3.5}{\percent} of rated), the stack at $j_\mathrm{min}$
plus the parasitic floor.

Fig.~\ref{fig:validation} shows the resulting polarization curve with its loss budget and
the system LHV efficiency. Cell voltage rises from \SI{1.507}{\volt} at
\SI{0.05}{\ampere\per\centi\meter\squared} to \SI{1.846}{\volt} at rated. Efficiency is
\emph{non-monotonic}: it peaks at \SI{70.5}{\percent} at
$j=\SI{0.73}{\ampere\per\centi\meter\squared}$, \SI{36}{\percent} of rated current, falling
to \SI{66.3}{\percent} at rated as voltage efficiency degrades and to \SI{31.9}{\percent} at
minimum load as crossover dominates. That non-monotonicity is why a controller has anything
to decide: the setpoint maximising hydrogen \emph{rate} is rated power, while the setpoint
maximising hydrogen \emph{per unit energy} is roughly one third of it.

\subsection{Degradation model}\label{sec:degmodel}
Degradation is represented as a single scalar $\dVdeg$, the cumulative cell-voltage rise in
microvolts, entering~\eqref{eq:vcell} directly. Physically this lumps combined ohmic and
kinetic degradation; it is the quantity manufacturers report, and recent reviews support it
as a reasonable summary of several distinct mechanisms~\cite{bernhardt2026degradation}.
Because it enters the polarization curve, accumulated degradation costs \emph{future yield}
and not merely reward: at a fixed available power a degraded stack settles at a lower
current density and produces less hydrogen.

Five mechanisms contribute per timestep, with $j_f=j/j_\mathrm{r}$: a steady-state base
rate $r_\mathrm{base}$~\cite{degmodels2025}; a high-current stress term
$k_j\max(0,j_f-0.6)^2$, since iridium dissolution and membrane thinning accelerate
superlinearly at high overpotential~\cite{feng2017pemwe,yu2020iridium}; a ramp term
$k_r|\mathrm{d}j/\mathrm{d}t|$ for thermal fatigue, independent of absolute
power~\cite{intermittency2025}; a fixed penalty $\Delta V_\mathrm{cycle}$ per on/off
transition, start/stop being the dominant intermittency
mechanism~\cite{intermittency2025}; and an idle term $r_\mathrm{idle}$ while off,
because the anode at open-circuit potential drives iridium
dissolution~\cite{feng2017pemwe,yu2020iridium}.

The idle term is load-bearing rather than incidental. Without it the reward-maximising
degenerate policy is to park at idle forever---no degradation, almost no hydrogen---and a
badly weighted run will find it. Including it is both physically correct and a structural
guard against the most likely reward-hacking failure.

\subsection{Calibration}\label{sec:calibration}
The five coefficients are solved for, not chosen, so that the degradation scale is anchored
to published numbers rather than invented ones.

With the shape parameters held fixed, each term is exactly linear in its coefficient, so
for any policy $p$ we have $\dVdeg(p)=\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{E}(p)$, where $\mathbf{E}(p)$
accumulates coefficient-free exposure integrals over a rollout. One rollout per policy
therefore reduces calibration to a small search in which every candidate is an exact dot
product. We require that (i) the literature rule-based controller
of~\cite{pvt_control_2026}, driven by the measured Kutch resource, reproduces
\SI{4.0}{\micro\volt\per\hour}, the $\approx$5-year stack life reported
in~\cite{mpc_refuel_2026} at a \SI{10}{\percent} (\SI{177}{\milli\volt}) end-of-life
criterion; (ii) a deliberately jittery policy degrades at least three times faster than a
smooth one, so the model can distinguish control strategies at all; (iii) no policy exceeds
the $\approx$\SI{50}{\micro\volt\per\hour} worst case reported under
cycling~\cite{intermittency2025}; (iv) every coefficient lies inside its published
interval; and (v) the separation is carried by \emph{both} intermittency mechanisms
of~\cite{intermittency2025}, ramping and on/off cycling, with neither below
\SI{20}{\percent} of the policy-dependent total. Condition (v) matters: without it a
coefficient set can satisfy the numerical targets while modelling only half the physics.

The solution places all five coefficients interior to their intervals
($r_\mathrm{base}=\SI{1.75}{\micro\volt\per\hour}$, $k_j=20.0$, $k_r=31.0$,
$\Delta V_\mathrm{cycle}=\SI{1.0}{\micro\volt}$,
$r_\mathrm{idle}=\SI{0.75}{\micro\volt\per\hour}$). The rule-based baseline lands at
\SI{4.00}{\micro\volt\per\hour}, a 5.05-year projected life, and the jittery/smooth
separation at $4.38\times$. Of the jittery policy's policy-dependent degradation, ramping
supplies \SI{63}{\percent} and cycling \SI{37}{\percent}, so both mechanisms are active.

Two caveats belong with this. First, the model is calibrated to literature-reported
\emph{aggregate} rates and is \emph{not} validated against a physical stack: it reproduces
a published lifetime and a published worst case and apportions them between five mechanisms
using published relative magnitudes, but it does not claim the apportionment is
individually correct. Second, $k_r$ sits near the top of its published interval, because
genuine one-minute cloud transients ramp the stack considerably harder than a smoothed
resource would; reproducing a five-year baseline life alongside a threefold separation
requires it. All conclusions drawn here are consequently about the \emph{relative}
lifetimes of different control policies, which is the quantity this apportionment supports.

\subsection{MDP formulation}
Control is formulated as a Markov decision process at a fixed interval of
$\Delta t = \SI{1}{\minute}$, with an episode spanning one day ($T = 1440$ steps).

\paragraph{Observation}
The agent observes an eight-dimensional vector, normalised to order unity: the available
renewable power $P_\mathrm{renew}(t)/P_\mathrm{r}$; its trailing 15-minute mean, which
supplies volatility context a single sample cannot; the previous power setpoint and the
present current density, both as fractions of rated; the accumulated degradation
$\dVdeg/\dVdeg^{\,\mathrm{EoL}}$; the instantaneous hydrogen rate; and the hour of day
encoded as $(\sin, \cos)$ so that the diurnal cycle is continuous at midnight.

\paragraph{Action}
A single continuous action $a_t \in [-1, 1]$, rescaled to a commanded power fraction and
then clipped to the resource:
\begin{equation}
P_\mathrm{set}(t) = \min\!\big(\tfrac{1}{2}(a_t + 1)\,P_\mathrm{r},\;
                               P_\mathrm{renew}(t)\big).
\label{eq:action}
\end{equation}
The plant is \textbf{renewable-only}: no grid import is permitted. This is a modelling
choice with consequences, and we state it explicitly because it defines the problem. Were
grid backfill allowed, the reward-optimal policy would collapse onto a fixed
peak-efficiency current density with the deficit purchased, the intermittency would leave
the problem entirely, and ``maximise yield'' would cease to be a meaningful objective.
Under the hard cap the question stays sharp: given the power that is available, how should
it be used? Grid-assisted operation is named in future work.

\paragraph{Reward}
\begin{equation}
r_t = w_1 \, \tilde m_{\mathrm{H_2}}(t) \;-\; w_2 \, \widetilde{\dVdeg}(t)
      \;-\; w_3 \, \frac{|\Delta j(t)|}{j_\mathrm{r}} ,
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde m_{\mathrm{H_2}}$ and $\widetilde{\dVdeg}$ are the per-step hydrogen mass and
degradation increment, each divided by a fixed scale so both terms are $O(1)$ per step. The
three components are logged \emph{unweighted and separately} throughout training. This is
not diagnostic convenience: total reward alone cannot distinguish a policy that is learning
the trade-off from one that has collapsed onto a degenerate corner, and both corners exist
here --- idling indefinitely, and tracking the resource to rated power at every instant.

\paragraph{Frontier}
$w_1$ and $w_3$ are held fixed and $w_2$ is swept, so each $w_2$ yields one point on the
yield--degradation frontier of Section~\ref{sec:results}. The sweep spans
$w_2 \in [0.1, 20]$ at 13 logarithmically spaced values. The upper limit is not arbitrary:
running is preferred to idling only while
$w_1\tilde m_{\mathrm{H_2}} > w_2(\widetilde{\dVdeg}^{\,\mathrm{run}} -
\widetilde{\dVdeg}^{\,\mathrm{idle}})$, which for this plant and resource gives
$w_2 \lesssim 20$. Beyond that the reward-optimal policy is to shut down, and the resulting
operating points are the degenerate corner rather than part of the frontier.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Resource data}
The plant is sited at \ang{23.25}\,N, \ang{69.00}\,E in Kutch, Gujarat, India, chosen
because it has strong solar \emph{and} strong wind resource at a single coordinate, so a
solar-heavy versus wind-heavy comparison is not confounded by geography.

cite ERA5~\cite{hersbach2020era5} and describe the PV~\cite{dobos2014pvwatts} and turbine
conversions as ours; if \texttt{renewables\_ninja}, cite~\cite{pfenninger2016} instead and
state that the conversions are the provider's.}

A full year of hourly data is upsampled to a one-minute interval, since the mechanisms of
Ref.~\cite{intermittency2025} are not resolved at hourly resolution. Sub-hourly structure
is added with an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck cloud-transient process on the solar component and a
turbulence process on the wind component; both are constrained to preserve each hourly
mean exactly, so no energy is introduced by the upsampling. Days are split
\num{293}/\num{72} into training and held-out test sets, stratified by month so that the
test set cannot fall entirely within one season --- a real confound at this site, which has
a pronounced monsoon.

\subsection{Training}
We train Soft Actor--Critic~\cite{haarnoja2018sac} and Proximal Policy
Optimization~\cite{schulman2017ppo} via Stable-Baselines3~\cite{raffin2021sb3}, both with
the default multilayer-perceptron policy. SAC is the primary algorithm --- the action space
is one-dimensional and continuous, where it is markedly more sample-efficient --- and PPO is
retained as an independent check, since a result that holds across two algorithm families
is a stronger claim than one obtained from a single optimiser.

Each configuration is trained for \num{2e6} environment steps on \num{32} parallel
environments drawn from the \num{293}-day training split, and evaluated on the
\num{72}-day held-out split. Every reported quantity is a mean over \num{5} random seeds,
with the standard deviation taken across seeds.

Two implementation details materially affect reproduction. First, SAC is run with
$\texttt{train\_freq} = \texttt{gradient\_steps} = n_\mathrm{env} = 32$. This batches the
updates while holding the update-to-step ratio at exactly $1{:}1$, which is the same
learning dynamics as the single-environment default at roughly $26\times$ the throughput;
reducing \texttt{gradient\_steps} below \texttt{train\_freq} would change the effective
algorithmic budget and is not used. Second, the two algorithms are placed on different
hardware for measured rather than assumed reasons: PPO runs on CPU and SAC on a GPU. This
workload is bound by Python environment stepping rather than by the network --- the policy
is small and the action space is one-dimensional --- so PPO is approximately
\SI{30}{\percent} \emph{slower} on the GPU than on CPU, while batched SAC gains about
$2.7\times$ from it.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

All figures report the mean over \num{5} random seeds, with error bars giving the standard
deviation across seeds, evaluated on the \num{72}-day held-out split.

\subsection{The published rule is dominated by removing one line}

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Effect of the idle threshold in the load-following rule of~\cite{pvt_control_2026},
on the held-out split}
\label{tab:threshold}
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
& H$_2$ & degradation & cycles & life \\
& [\si{\kilo\gram\per\day}] & [\si{\micro\volt\per\day}] & [\si{\per\day}] & [\si{\year}] \\
\midrule
As published ($P_\mathrm{idle}$ at \SI{10}{\percent}) & 201.8 & 96.9 & 22.9 & 5.00 \\
Threshold removed                                     & \textbf{205.0} & \textbf{82.7}
                                                      & \textbf{7.0}  & \textbf{5.86} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Before assessing what learning contributes, it is worth establishing how much of the
available improvement requires no learning at all. The load-following law
of~\cite{pvt_control_2026} idles the stack whenever the resource falls below a threshold,
set at \SI{10}{\percent} of rated. Removing that threshold --- so the plant follows the
resource down instead of shutting off --- Pareto-dominates the published rule on the
held-out split (Table~\ref{tab:threshold}): \SI{1.6}{\percent} \emph{more} hydrogen and
\SI{14.7}{\percent} \emph{less} degradation simultaneously, from \SI{69}{\percent} fewer
ON/OFF events.

We report this for two reasons. It quantifies the cost of the cycling mechanism
independently of any controller comparison, which is what makes the mechanism in
Section~\ref{sec:results} concrete rather than asserted. And it sets the standard for an
honest baseline: a study comparing a learned policy only against the rule as published
would be crediting learning with an improvement obtainable by deleting a conditional. The
tuned slew-limited controller used throughout the rest of this paper is a considerably
stronger comparison, and we recommend it as the reference for future work on this problem.

\subsection{The yield--degradation frontier}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_pareto.pdf}
\caption{Hydrogen yield against cumulative degradation on held-out days. The learned policy
traces a frontier as the degradation weight $w_2$ is swept over 13 values; the two
rule-based controllers are single operating points. The frontier extends to degradation
rates neither rule reaches, but does not dominate either of them.}
\label{fig:pareto}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto} shows the central result. Sweeping $w_2$ from \num{0.1} to \num{20}
moves the learned policy smoothly and monotonically along a frontier spanning
\SIrange{191.8}{195.9}{\kilo\gram\per\day} of hydrogen and
\SIrange{65.6}{84.5}{\micro\volt\per\day} of degradation --- a \SI{2.1}{\percent} range in
yield against a \SI{22.3}{\percent} range in degradation. The asymmetry is itself
informative: at this plant scale and resource, the controller has far more leverage over
how fast the stack ages than over how much hydrogen it makes.

\textbf{The learned policy does not dominate the tuned baseline, and we do not claim that
it does.} Of the 13 frontier points, none is simultaneously better than either rule-based
controller on both axes. Interpolating the frontier to the ramp-limited baseline's
degradation of \SI{78.3}{\micro\volt\per\day}, the learned policy yields
\SI{191.8}{\kilo\gram\per\day} against the baseline's \num{199.5} --- the baseline is
\SI{4.0}{\percent} better at that operating point. A well-tuned slew limit is a strong
controller for this problem, and a study that reported only a comparison against the naive
load-following rule would have overstated what learning contributes.

What the learned policy does provide is \emph{reach}. The lowest degradation any
rule-based controller attains is \SI{78.3}{\micro\volt\per\day}, a projected stack life of
\SI{6.20}{\year}. At $w_2=20$ the learned policy reaches
\SI{65.6}{\micro\volt\per\day} --- \SI{16.2}{\percent} lower, a projected
\SI{7.39}{\year}, an increase of \SI{19.3}{\percent} in service life --- for
\SI{3.9}{\percent} less hydrogen. That operating point is not reachable by tightening the
slew limit: doing so makes the controller too sluggish to follow the resource and costs
yield faster than it saves degradation (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). The contribution of
learning here is to extend the attainable trade-off, not to dominate it.

\subsection{Mechanism: cycle avoidance bought with curtailment}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_mechanism.pdf}
\caption{What the learned policy does differently. Left: ON/OFF cycles per day fall
monotonically as $w_2$ rises. Right: the price, in deliberately curtailed energy.
Horizontal lines are the two rule-based controllers.}
\label{fig:mechanism}
\end{figure*}

Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism} explains the frontier rather than asserting it. As $w_2$ rises the
learned policy reduces ON/OFF cycling from \num{10.7} to \num{2.0} events per day, against
\num{22.9} for the load-following rule of~\cite{pvt_control_2026} and \num{9.2} for the
tuned slew limit. It pays for this with curtailment, which rises from
\SIrange{420}{694}{\kilo\watt\hour\per\day}.

This identifies the operative mechanism. At this site the resource averages
\SI{31}{\percent} of rated power, so the stack spends most of its operating time well below
the current-density threshold at which the high-overpotential stress term activates.
Uniformly reducing power therefore saves very little degradation while costing yield
immediately --- and an agent that simply tracks the resource at maximum power is behaving
correctly under this reward. The degradation a controller can actually influence is
dominated by \emph{cycling}, and the learned policy discovers that declining power to stay
above the ON threshold through a resource dip is worth more than the hydrogen forgone. The
rule-based controller cannot make that trade: its idle threshold is a fixed rule, so it
shuts down whenever the resource falls below it and pays the restart cost every time.

\subsection{Algorithm choice is not incidental}

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Response to the degradation weight, identical MDP and budget}
\label{tab:algos}
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & yield span & degradation span & behaviour \\
\midrule
SAC & \SI{2.1}{\percent} & \SI{22.3}{\percent} & traces the frontier \\
PPO & \SI{0.0}{\percent} & \SI{0.0}{\percent} & maximum-power tracking \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Trained on the same environment for the same \num{2e6} steps, PPO does not respond to
$w_2$ at all. Its policies at $w_2 = \num{0.1}$ and $w_2 = \num{20}$ are identical to the
reported precision, operating at the available power on \SI{100}{\percent} of ON steps
(Table~\ref{tab:algos}).

The difference is explanatory rather than incidental. Maximum-power tracking is a good
local policy: it is optimal over most of the weight range, and departing from it costs
yield immediately while the degradation saving accrues only through reduced cycling later
in the episode. On-policy updates offer no pressure to leave such a policy once reached.
The maximum-entropy objective of SAC~\cite{haarnoja2018sac} continues to reward action
diversity, keeping the policy in the curtailment region long enough for the cycling benefit
to become visible in the returns. We report this because it bears directly on
reproduction: on this problem, the choice of an off-policy, entropy-regularised algorithm
is a precondition for observing the trade-off at all, not a tuning preference.

\subsection{Long-horizon operation}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_longhorizon.pdf}
\caption{Projected stack life under the two rule-based controllers. Solid segments are the
\num{90}-day rollout with persistent degradation state (inset, at its own scale); dotted
lines extrapolate linearly at the measured rate to the \SI{10}{\percent} voltage-rise
end-of-life criterion. The extrapolation is a projection at the measured rate, not a
prediction: degradation rates are not constant over a stack's service life.}
\label{fig:longhorizon}
\end{figure}

Over the \num{90}-day rollout with degradation persisting across days, the naive
load-following law produces \SI{17144}{\kilo\gram} of hydrogen while degrading at
\SI{4.05}{\micro\volt\per\hour}, a projected life of \SI{4.99}{\year}; adding a slew limit
yields \SI{16955}{\kilo\gram} at \SI{3.20}{\micro\volt\per\hour}, or \SI{6.31}{\year}.

This is also an independent check on the calibration of Section~\ref{sec:calibration}. The
degradation coefficients were fitted so that the rule-based baseline reproduces the
approximately five-year service life reported in~\cite{mpc_refuel_2026}, using single-day
episodes on the training split. The \num{90}-day persistent rollout (\SI{4.99}{\year}) and
the \num{72}-day held-out evaluation (\SI{5.00}{\year}) reproduce that figure under two
protocols the coefficients were not fitted to.

We are precise about what this does and does not establish. All three protocols share
the same degradation equations, so the agreement shows the calibration is \emph{stable}
--- not an artifact of the particular days it was fitted on, nor of the single-day
episode structure --- but it is not evidence that those equations describe a physical
stack. No measurement of a real device enters this work at any point. The absolute
lifetimes we report are pinned to a single published figure~\cite{mpc_refuel_2026}; if
that figure is wrong, every absolute lifetime here scales with it, while the relative
comparisons between controllers are unaffected because they would all shift together.

\subsection{Ablation: the reward weighting}

\begin{figure*}[t]

standard deviation over \num{5} seeds.}

Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation} shows both reward components across the sweep. The degradation term
responds strongly and monotonically while the yield term is nearly flat, which is the

The sweep range terminates at $w_2 = 20$ by construction. Above approximately that value
the reward-optimal policy is to shut the plant down entirely: running is preferred to
idling only while $w_1 \tilde m_{\mathrm{H_2}} > w_2(\widetilde{\dVdeg}^{\,\mathrm{run}} -
\widetilde{\dVdeg}^{\,\mathrm{idle}})$, which for this plant and resource gives
$w_2 \lesssim 20$. Weights beyond that produce the degenerate corner rather than frontier
points, and are excluded.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We formulated real-time setpoint control of a \SI{1}{\mega\watt} PEM electrolyzer under
direct renewable supply as a Markov decision process at a one-minute control interval, with
a five-term degradation model coupled back into the polarization curve so that accumulated
degradation reduces future yield. The degradation coefficients were calibrated so that the
literature rule-based controller reproduces a published stack lifetime, and that
calibration was then confirmed under two evaluation protocols it was not fitted to.

Three findings follow.

First, the controllable degradation at this plant scale is dominated by \emph{ON/OFF
cycling} rather than by operating power. The resource averages \SI{31}{\percent} of rated,
so the stack rarely reaches the current density at which high-overpotential stress
activates, and uniformly reducing power costs yield while saving little. A learned policy
that maximises power is behaving correctly under this reward; the trade-off lives in
cycling and ramping.

Second, a learned policy extends the attainable trade-off without dominating a well-tuned
rule. Sweeping the degradation weight traces a frontier spanning \SI{22.3}{\percent} in
degradation against \SI{2.1}{\percent} in yield, reaching \SI{65.6}{\micro\volt\per\day}
--- a projected \SI{7.39}{\year} of service life against \SI{6.20}{\year} for the best
rule-based controller --- for \SI{3.9}{\percent} less hydrogen. At the operating point the
two share, the tuned slew limit is \SI{4.0}{\percent} better on yield. The value of
learning here is reach, not dominance, and we think reporting that distinction plainly is
more useful to a practitioner than a single headline ratio.

Third, obtaining this result required an off-policy, entropy-regularised algorithm. PPO
trained on the identical environment for the identical budget did not respond to the
degradation weight at all, remaining at maximum-power tracking across the entire sweep.

\paragraph{Future work}
The most valuable next step is a direct comparison against model predictive control, which
is the natural competitor and is discussed as a limitation below rather than claimed as a
contribution here. Beyond that: allowing grid-assisted operation, which changes the
objective qualitatively; a thermal state in the plant model, which would let ramp-rate
penalties emerge from dynamics rather than being posited; and validation of the degradation
model against a measured trajectory from a physical stack.

\subsection*{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
Four, stated plainly rather than left for a reviewer to find.

First, this is a \emph{simulation study}. No physical stack was operated, and the plant
model, while parameterised from published sources, is not validated against measurement
from the device it represents.

Second, the degradation model is \emph{calibrated from literature-reported rates} rather
than fitted to a measured degradation trajectory. Its absolute scale is anchored to a
single published lifetime figure~\cite{mpc_refuel_2026}; the relative weighting of its five
terms follows the mechanisms of Ref.~\cite{intermittency2025} but is not independently
identified. Conclusions about the \emph{relative} performance of controllers are therefore
considerably more robust than any absolute lifetime we report, and the projected-life
figures should be read as a consistent scale for comparison rather than as forecasts.

Third, we do not compare against model predictive control. MPC is the natural competitor
and has real advantages this work does not claim to displace --- interpretability, and hard
constraint satisfaction by construction. The argument for reinforcement learning here is
narrower: it requires no explicit dynamics or degradation model at deployment, it can be
trained offline on historical resource data, and it does not re-solve an optimisation at
every control step, which matters at a one-minute interval.

Fourth, the results are for a single site and a single plant configuration. The mechanism
we identify --- that cycling dominates when the resource sits well below rated power ---
should be expected to weaken at sites where the plant is more often near its rated point,
and the balance between the degradation terms would shift accordingly.
